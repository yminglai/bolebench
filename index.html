What

Can an LLM judge which research approach will win — before the results exist?

Bole shows judges only the ex-ante description of competing research attempts. Reality — many systems attempting the same task under the same budget — settles who was right. No committee votes, no citation proxies: outcomes resolve every item.

Scope, honestly: BoleBench measures the taste inside running research — pick, kill, dare, trust — the layer AI systems already exercise with real compute. Which problems deserve to exist is a different, harder question we do not claim. And the item bank is renewable by design: vintage-2 (23 fresh tasks × 5 systems, the judges now also the judged) is generating as you read. Scores expire with each model generation; the measurement procedure does not.

How

  1. Blinded pairs. Two attempts at the same ML research task, described up to the decision point — identity, scores, and results stripped.
  2. Judges predict. Models (or you) pick the attempt that achieved the higher final score, with a confidence.
  3. Reality resolves. Scoring uses gap-stratified accuracy, calibration, and selection regret — not raw win rate, which rewards whoever controls the item pool.

Sources · benchmark-agnostic by construction

Any corpus with one problem, two or more attempts, and one reality-resolved number can feed the bank — existing RSI benchmarks, synthetic tasks forged from papers, papers racing their own ablations, or live proposals resolved later. Same converter, same judges, one leaderboard.

Findings so far

  • One Bo Le for the whole field. Across 8 judges from 4 model families, 4.5% of items are missed by every judge (4.9% raw; net of 9 audited bank defects) — ≈135× what independent errors would predict. Shared training begets shared taste, and shared blind spots.
  • Taste is worth real compute. The best judge, picking 1 of 10 real attempts, captures 75% of the oracle–random gap. The worst judge is below random: an unevaluated research preference model can be worse than a coin.
  • Judges obey evidence, they don't weigh it. When exploration evidence points the wrong way (28% of the time), accuracy collapses to 23–45%. Trust is a distinct, measurable sub-skill.
  • Most of frontier taste is one instinct. "Pick the bolder attempt" scores 77.9%, beating every judge. Strip the pairs that instinct answers and judges keep +10–14 points of residual judgment — thin, real, and where models actually differ.
  • Taste is not one skill. One of the weakest pickers is the best at knowing what to kill; four sub-skills — pick, kill, dare, trust — measurably dissociate.
  • The signal is shallow but real. A 350-character gist captures most of the description signal; process evidence adds up to +18 points; author identity adds little for most judges — it is genuinely informative, judges just barely use it.
