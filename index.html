What

Can an LLM judge which research approach will win — before the results exist?

Bole shows judges only what each attempt looked like before it was run. Reality — many systems attempting the same task under the same budget — settles who was right. No committee votes, no citation proxies: outcomes resolve every item.

Scope, honestly: BoleBench measures the taste inside running research — pick, kill, dare, trust — the layer AI systems already exercise with real compute. Which problems deserve to exist is a different, harder question we do not claim. And the item bank is renewable by design: a second batch of items (23 fresh tasks × 5 systems, the judges now also the judged) is generating as you read. Scores expire with each model generation; the measurement procedure does not.

How

  1. Blinded pairs. Two attempts at the same ML research task, described up to the decision point — identity, scores, and results stripped.
  2. Judges predict. Models (or you) pick the attempt that achieved the higher final score, with a confidence.
  3. Reality resolves. Scoring splits accuracy by how close each race was, checks whether stated confidence is honest, and measures what following a judge would actually cost — not raw win rate, which rewards whoever controls the item pool.

How tasks are admitted

Any benchmark can supply BoleBench items if its tasks pass six criteria — the selection standard is the benchmark's identity, stated before anything is rolled out:

  1. The score is hidden and objective. A held-out metric the attempt cannot see; no LLM-judge or rubric scoring.
  2. Several systems run the same task. Same frozen start, same environment — so the failures and the paths not taken are all on record.
  3. Real room to decide. An expert baseline to beat and more than one viable approach; tasks where attempts mostly tie are dropped.
  4. Describable before the results. The approach can be written down without giving away how it turned out, so blinding is possible.
  5. Datable. Every outcome can be dated against each judge's training cutoff, so memorization is testable.
  6. Equal budgets. Every system gets the same compute and time, so the race is fair.

Admitted tasks are rolled out (k systems × matched budgets); items then pass a second gate: ties removed, pairs sorted by how close the race was, blinding plus a leak check, committee labels. AI4AI and RSI-Exam pass all six; PaperBench fails the hidden-score rule (it uses LLM-judge scoring); GAIA fails the describe-before-results rule. In a 28-work audit of this field, 25 build hybrid banks from borrowed material and none author items from scratch — sourcing is the norm, the selection standard is what differs.

Sources → item bank → pairwise comparison

Items come from a few kinds of sources — existing benchmarks (e.g. AI4AI-Bench), arXiv papers racing their own ablations, and anything else with several real attempts at one goal. They all become the same thing: a blinded item bank, judged by pairwise comparison, resolved by reality.

Findings so far

  • One Bo Le for the whole field. Across 8 judges from 4 model families, 4.5% of items are missed by every judge (4.9% raw; net of 9 audited bank defects) — ≈135× what independent errors would predict. Shared training begets shared taste, and shared blind spots.
  • Taste is worth real compute. The best judge, picking 1 of 10 real attempts, closes 75% of the gap between random picking and always picking the winner. The worst judge is below random: an unevaluated research preference model can be worse than a coin.
  • Judges obey evidence, they don't weigh it. When exploration evidence points the wrong way (28% of the time), accuracy collapses to 23–45%. Trust is a distinct, measurable sub-skill.
  • Most of frontier taste is one instinct. "Pick the bolder attempt" scores 77.9%, beating every judge. Strip the pairs that instinct answers and judges keep +10–14 points of residual judgment — thin, real, and where models actually differ.
  • Taste is not one skill. One of the weakest pickers is the best at knowing what to kill; four sub-skills — pick, kill, dare, trust — measurably dissociate.
  • The signal is shallow but real. A 350-character gist captures most of the description signal; process evidence adds up to +18 points; author identity adds little for most judges — it is genuinely informative, judges just barely use it.
